Unpacking the Links between Conflict and Child Health: Evidence from a Foreign Insurgency, 
  (with <a href="https://sites.google.com/view/heidikaila/home">Heidi Kaila</a> and Larissa Nawo), 
  <i>Economic Development and Cultural Change</i>, July 2026, 74(4).
  <a href="https://doi.org/10.1086/740156"> [Link] </a>
</summary>
<div class="text-box">
<p style="text-align: left; background-color: #F1F1F1; border: 7px solid white;">
<b>Abstract </b>
<br>
  Violent conflict has enduring effects on child health, but the speed at which these effects manifest is not fully understood. This study investigates the immediate effects of deteriorating security environment caused by foreign-borne insurgent terrorism on children’s health, using data from a decade before to shortly after the Nigerian Boko Haram insurgency extended across the border to Cameroon. Boko Haram at- tacks decrease weight-for-height for children under five – an indicator of short-term health and nutrition – within an average of 2.6 months after the attacks. This effect is likely driven by a reduction in healthcare service utilization, which can exacerbate the prevalence and the severity of conditions such as fever and diarrhea. However, the attacks do not affect dietary diversity or child mortality. The results underscore the importance of maintaining accessibility to healthcare service after the start of terrorist violence to prevent irreversible impacts, a concern that is increasingly relevant for countries combating the infiltration of foreign terrorists.
 </p>
</div>

<summary>
  When Student Incentives Don't Work: Evidence from a Field Experiment in Malawi, 
  (with <a href="https://sites.google.com/site/econjimberry/">James Berry</a> and <a href="https://sites.google.com/site/hk2405/home">Hyuncheol Bryant Kim</a>), 
  <i>Journal of Development Economics</i>, September 2022. 158(2022). 
  <a href="http://hyukhson.github.io/files/Berry_et_al_JDE_2022.pdf"> [Link] </a>
</summary>
<div class="text-box">
<p style="text-align: left; background-color: #F1F1F1; border: 7px solid white;">
<b>Abstract </b>
<br>
We study how the structure of tournament incentive schemes in education can influence the level and distribution of student outcomes. Through a field experiment among upper-primary students in Malawi, we evaluate two scholarship programs: a Population-based scholarship that rewarded overall top performers on an exam and a Bin-based scholarship that rewarded the top performers within smaller groups of students with similar baseline scores. We find that the Population-based scholarship decreased test scores and motivation to study, especially for those least likely to win. By contrast, we find no evidence for test score impacts among those in the Bin-based scholarship program.  
 </p>
</div>

</summary>
<div class="text-box">
<p style="text-align: left; background-color: #F1F1F1; border: 7px solid white;">
<b>Abstract </b>
<br>
We describe a unique environment in which undergraduate students from various STEM and social science disciplines are trained in data provenance and reproducible methods, and then apply that knowledge to real, conditionally accepted manuscripts and associated replication packages. We describe in detail the recruitment, training, and regular activities. While the activity is not part of a regular curriculum, the skills and knowledge taught through explicit training of reproducible methods and principles, and reinforced through repeated application in a real-life workflow, contribute to the education of these undergraduate students, and prepare them for post-graduation jobs and further studies. Supplementary materials for this article are available online.
 </p>

Long-run Effects of Temporary Catastrophic Drought Insurance Coverage, 
(with <a href="http://barrett.dyson.cornell.edu/">Chris Barrett</a>, <a href="https://sites.google.com/cornell.edu/nathan-jensen/home">Nathan Jensen</a>, <a href="https://www.karlijnmorsink.com/">Karlijn Morsink</a>, Yuma Norimoto, Rupsha Banerjee, and Nils Teufel)
<i>, R&R at American Economic Review </i> 
  <a href="https://hyukhson.github.io/files/Long_run_Effects_of_Catastrophic_Drought_Insurance.pdf"> [Link] </a>,
  <a href="https://www.financialprotectionforum.org/blog/pilot-scale-how-research-powers-pastoralists-progress"> [Blog] </a>
</summary>
<div class="text-box">
<p style="text-align: left; background-color: #F1F1F1; border: 7px solid white;">
<b>Abstract </b>
<br>
  We study the long-run effects of temporary catastrophic drought insurance coverage for pastoralists in Kenya and Ethiopia, instrumenting purchase with randomized premium discounts and resurveying households a decade later. As predicted by multiple equilibrium poverty-trap models, effects diverge by baseline herd size. Among smaller-herd households, insurance
reduced holdings of goats and sheep, a precautionary reserve, and sharply raised children’s education, consistent with reduced demand for child herding labor. Among larger-herd households, insurance increased holdings of higher-return large animals, with no comparable effect on schooling. These effects persisted or grew, suggesting temporary risk reduction can durably
change household investment.
 </p>
</div>

<summary>
Reducing Child Labor through Risk Management: Short-Term Behavioral Effects of Index Insurance in East Africa,
  <i>submitted</i> 
  <a href="https://hyukhson.github.io/files/ibli_childlabor.pdf"> [Link] </a>
</summary>
<div class="text-box">
<p style="text-align: left; background-color: #F1F1F1; border: 7px solid white;">
<b>Abstract </b>
<br>
  This paper examines how drought insurance affects children's work and schooling in pastoralist households in Kenya and Ethiopia. Using randomized premium discounts to instrument purchases of index-based livestock insurance, I find that an additional purchase across three relevant sales seasons reduces child labor by 11.0 percentage points and any reported work by 12.7 points. Children are less likely to combine work and schooling and more likely to report schooling without work, with no detectable increase in enrollment. The reduction is concentrated in secondary work. Insurance also increases the share of livestock at satellite camps by 15.3 percentage points, especially outside drought, without a detectable increase in recorded child movement. These parallel responses are consistent with changes in the spatial organization of production. The findings show that subsidized climate-risk protection can reduce children's work alongside schooling, through spatial rearrangement of production strategies.
 </p>
</div>

<summary>
  Paying Agents to Promote Consumer Welfare from Catastrophic Drought Insurance, 
(with <a href="http://barrett.dyson.cornell.edu/">Chris Barrett</a>, Tagel Gabrehiwot Gidey,  <a href="https://sites.google.com/cornell.edu/nathan-jensen/home">Nathan Jensen</a>, <a href="https://www.karlijnmorsink.com/">Karlijn Morsink</a>, and Max Yap)
 4<a href="https://hyukhson.github.io/files/paying_agents_to_promote_consumer_welfare.pdf"> [Link] </a> 
</summary>
<div class="text-box">
<p style="text-align: left; background-color: #F1F1F1; border: 7px solid white;">
<b>Abstract </b>
<br>
Personalized, data-driven financial advice is increasingly feasible at scale, but whether it improves consumer welfare depends on an underexplored condition: the incentive structure of the intermediaries who deliver it. We study this in a cluster-randomized trial with 2,416 households and 80 commissioned insurance agents in Ethiopia. We cross-randomize the provision of individualized welfare-based recommendations with an alternative agent compensation scheme that rewards alignment with those recommendations rather than sales volume. We develop a structural model to compute household-specific expected consumer surplus (ECS) from index-based drought insurance in real time and use it to generate tailored purchase advice. Despite 85% of households having positive expected welfare gains from insurance, only 8% purchase. Personalized advice alone has no detectable effect on take-up or ECS under standard revenue-based agent incentives. When advice is paired with welfare-aligned incentives, insurance take-up declines by 12%, welfare-reducing purchases fall by 25%, and expected consumer surplus rises by 45% among those advised not to purchase. Effects are concentrated among households with low baseline product knowledge — those most susceptible to agent overselling. Technological advances in personalized prediction do not, by themselves, expand the welfare frontier: realizing their potential requires complementary reforms to the incentive structures governing financial intermediation.
</p>
</div>  
</details>

<summary>
Gold Mine Openings and Child Labor in Mali,
<a href="http://hyukhson.github.io/files/gold_mine_child_labor.pdf"> [Link] </a>
</summary>
<div class="text-box">
<p style="text-align: left; background-color: #F1F1F1; border: 7px solid white;">
<b>Abstract </b>
<br>
  This study investigates the effect of a natural resource shock on child labor using the opening dates and the location of the industrial gold mines in Mali. Unlike other papers that show mines increase children’s work, I find that the opening of mines decreases children’s work, specifically the working hours for household tasks while it does not affect the school enrollments. The effects were heterogeneous by age and birth order. I claim that my results stem from the income effects of the mines dominating the substitution effects by presenting the evidence on the adults’ employment and occupational choices.
 </p>
